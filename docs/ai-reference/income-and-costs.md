# Income and costs

Income is money the portfolio received without selling anything: dividends, coupon and interest
payments, and distributions. It is recorded on the date it settled, in the currency it arrived in,
and converted at that day's rate for a report in base currency.

A dividend is paid to a deposit account. Under a securities-only lens it is out of view — see the
scope topic; this is the single most common reason income "disappears".

Withholding tax is its own operation, not a smaller dividend. Gross income and net income therefore
differ, and both are real numbers: the gross is what the instrument distributed, the net is what
arrived.

Costs come in two shapes, and the difference is the same one lots make: a commission paid on a
purchase is part of what the shares cost, while a standalone fee or tax is a charge. A question
about fees paid over a period means charges; a question about what the portfolio costs to run means
both, expressed as a share of the average value.

A commission and a tax do not have to be in the same currency as the operation they belong to: a
commission is charged where the trade was executed and withholding is deducted where the issuer
sits. Each is then converted at the rate of its own currency on the operation's date, and the
money leaves the balance held in that currency rather than the one the trade settled in. The
figure is not an estimate made today — it is fixed at the day it was paid, like every other
converted amount.

Neither income nor costs are a return on their own. Income is *part* of total return, alongside
price movement; a yield computed from income and value is a separate figure and should be named as
such rather than compared to the return.

## Income under a classification tree

Income can be read through the same trees that classify value, and the two answer different
questions. A category's share of value is what it is worth today; a category's income is what its
members paid over a window. A payment is filed under whoever paid it — the instrument for a
dividend, the account's cash balance for interest — and an instrument split across categories
splits its payments by the same weights.

A category can hold a great deal and pay nothing, so it is absent from the income split rather
than present at zero, and an instrument sold during the window still counts what it paid while
held. A category switched off in a tree is outside both the split and its total; whatever the tree
never classified is gathered as unclassified.

## Expected dividends

The expected dividends list is a forecast and writes nothing. It covers the instruments held
today, and only those whose price source reports dividends — an instrument priced by hand, or one
whose source reports none, is never in it, which does not mean it pays nothing.

Each payment the source reported over the instrument's last year is carried one year forward. A
monthly or quarterly payer is assumed to pay its latest amount every time, so a recent raise
shows; a half-yearly or yearly payer repeats each payment's own amount, because an interim and a
final dividend differ. A payment more than three times the year's typical one is treated as a
special dividend and not repeated. A payer silent for more than two of its usual gaps is treated
as suspended and dropped.

The amount is today's shares times the amount per share, at today's exchange rate. A purchase or
sale before the ex-date is not foreseen. The date shown is the expected payment date when the
portfolio has received this instrument's dividends before (the usual delay after the ex-date is
learned from them); otherwise it is the ex-date. Likewise the amount is net of the withholding the
last received payment had, and gross when none was ever received. *Announced* means the source
already reported that payment and only the cash is still due; *estimated* means the date and
amount are carried forward and may change.
