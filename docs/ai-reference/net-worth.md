# Net worth, assets and liabilities

Two totals exist and they are not interchangeable.

- **Portfolio value** is what is invested: instruments at their market price plus the cash on the
  accounts. Every return, every risk figure, every allocation and every rebalance plan is about
  this total and only this total.
- **Net worth** is portfolio value plus everything else owned, minus everything owed. It is a
  balance sheet, not a performance.

Nothing owned or owed outside the portfolio enters a return, a volatility, a Sharpe ratio, an
allocation weight or a rebalance trade. When a question mixes the two — "how did my wealth do this
year" — the honest answer separates them: the portfolio returned *x*, and net worth changed by *y*,
of which part is a revaluation somebody typed and part is money that moved.

## What these things are

An asset here is something with no market to price it: property, a vehicle, a collectible, a
private company share, money lent out, or cash held somewhere with no ledger in the app. A
liability is a debt: a mortgage, a loan, a credit card, a drawn credit line, tax assessed and
unpaid.

Both are stored as positive amounts; which way an amount points is a property of its kind, not a
sign. So a mortgage of 250 000 is the figure 250 000, subtracted when the total is formed.

Three things are deliberately *not* modelled this way:

- **An instrument with a published price** is part of the portfolio, not an asset here. Gold with
  a quote is an instrument; gold in a safe with an opinion attached is an asset.
- **Something operations are recorded against** is an account. The dividing line is how it is
  kept: entries and transfers make it an account, periodic restatement makes it an asset. Only
  accounts can carry contribution limits, because a contribution is money that entered the
  portfolio, and a revaluation is neither money nor an account.
- **Margin debt at a broker** is negative cash on an account. It belongs to invested capital, and
  moving it out here would distort the returns it legitimately affects.

## Dated valuations, read as steps

Every figure is dated, and the value in force on any day is the latest figure dated on or before
it. There is no interpolation: between two valuations nothing was measured, so the value is the
older one, unchanged, and the net-worth line is a staircase. A thing is absent before its first
valuation — which is not the same as being worth zero — and absent from its closing day onwards,
while its history remains readable.

Figures are converted to the base currency at the **reading date's** exchange rate, like every
other figure shown in the base currency, not at the rate of the day the valuation was written. A
missing rate makes the reading fail rather than counting the thing as nothing.

## What it can and cannot answer

It can answer what is owned and owed, what the total is, how much of it is invested, when each
figure was last written, and what is still waiting for its first valuation.

It cannot answer how a flat "performed", what the equity in it is worth after selling costs, or
what a debt will be next year — the interest rate and monthly payment on a debt describe the
schedule ahead and never restate the balance. A valuation months old makes net worth quietly
stale; the age of each figure is reported, and an answer that leans on an old one should say so.
